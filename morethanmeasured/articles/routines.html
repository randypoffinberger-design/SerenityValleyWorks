When routines help and when they need flexibility

More Than Measured Resource Library · September 2026

A good routine answers “what happens next?” without requiring every minute of the day to be identical.

Build anchors, not a minute-by-minute script

Morning, leaving home, meals, after-school time and bedtime are useful places for repeatable sequences. Keep the number of steps manageable. If a routine needs twenty reminders, simplify it.

Make the next step visible

Pictures, written lists, objects, timers or a dry-erase board can move information out of the caregiver's voice and into the environment. This can reduce repeated prompting and make expectations easier to revisit.

Preview known changes

If an appointment will replace the normal afternoon activity, show that change before it happens when possible. A “change” card or simple explanation can be part of the routine itself.

Practice small flexibility

Flexibility does not have to mean surprise. A familiar routine can occasionally include two acceptable choices or a planned small variation. The goal is to expand tolerance gradually, not manufacture distress.

Track which transitions are actually hard

Instead of assuming all change is difficult, note the time, activity being stopped, next activity, warning given and what helped. You may discover that one transition is consistently harder because of hunger, fatigue or losing access to a favorite activity.

Keep it practical: the best routine is one the household can actually repeat. A simpler plan used consistently is usually more useful than an elaborate system that disappears after three days.
