MEALS & FOOD

Making mealtimes more predictable

More Than Measured Resource Library · September 2026

Food can involve taste, smell, temperature, texture, appearance, motor skills and predictability all at once. Tracking what works can be more useful than simply labeling a child “picky.”

Separate eating from exposure

A new food can be present without requiring a bite. Seeing, smelling, touching or tolerating a food nearby may be part of becoming familiar with it. Pressure can make an already difficult sensory experience harder.

Keep something familiar available

When practical, include at least one food the child commonly accepts alongside family foods. That can make the meal feel safer while still allowing exposure to variety.

Look for patterns

Record characteristics of accepted foods: crunchy or soft, dry or saucy, hot or cold, separated or mixed, specific brands or shapes. Patterns can reveal what matters and help identify small changes that may be tolerated.

Change one variable at a time

If a familiar food changes brand, shape and seasoning at once, it may no longer feel familiar. Small changes make it easier to understand what caused acceptance or refusal.

Products should reduce friction

A preferred cup, utensil, plate or storage container can be useful when it solves a real issue. Avoid buying a large set before knowing whether size, weight, texture or shape actually matters to the child.

Health note: choking, swallowing difficulty, poor growth, dehydration, nutritional concerns or an extremely limited diet should be discussed with qualified healthcare professionals. This guide is not medical or nutrition advice.
